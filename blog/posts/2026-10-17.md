# Japan Trip - Day 7

Today was a bit of a long day. I had planned to take a longer trip, going from the city of Amakusa to the city of Izumi. I got up a bit earlier than usual, since I had to get breakfast on the road. I immediately went to a gas station down the road, and it was my first time running into a gas station that wasn't self-serve. No problem, I figured it out even without understanding a word of the Japanese being used.

Next, I went to a family mart, which was just outside the hotel that I was staying at. I got a quick grab-n-go breakfast of an egg, ham and tomato sandwich, a tuna-mayo onigiri (my favorite) and a water and coffee. I decided to take it to go, and eat it on the road. I didn't make it 5 minutes before ending up on a heavily shaded road, so I pulled over and ate my quick meal in the middle of a dense forest, with a stream running nearby. This isn't the first time I've been able to just randomly pull over and end up in a gorgeous location, it's just so easy to do that here in Kyushu.

## Nishibira Camellia Park

My first destination today was a direct recommendation from the family that I met last night. They showed me a picture of an absolutely incredible tree, which had wild roots, and looked like you gave a tree magic powers to grow. I thought it looked incredible, so I headed there, expecting to find this tree in the middle of a dense forest, with nothing much else around. 

![A picture of a viny tree, wrapped around a stone, surrounded by trees and moss](/blog/images/nishibiraTree.JPG)

That's not what this was though, in actuality, the tree is only a minor part of the park. There are trees throughout the park which bloom beautiful red flowers in the spring (I wasn't able to see the blooms, but I did see pictures), and the park was on a cliff side, right by the ocean. The ocean was some of the clearest aquamarine waters that I have ever seen, and there were rock formations jutting out of the waters not too far from the coast, making the view look like some of the woodblock paintings that I've seen in museums. 

![Rock formations in the ocean, protruding from the waters, framed by rocks and a chain fence](/blog/images/nishibiraOcean.JPG)

The walk to the tree, and to the viewing point of the ocean, was super easy, just walking down stairs. However, the walk back was killer, since I had no choice but to walk directly up the stairs that I had just descended. By the time that I made it back to my bike, I was sweating pretty badly, and the sun was cooking both me, my bike, and the gear on my bike. As nice as the view was, and how much I liked the vibe of the location, I needed to get moving again, if just to get air moving through my jacket.

## Sakitsu Church & village

My next destination was Sakitsu Church, which wasn't too far away. The ride there was pretty smooth, I didn't even really have to change roads on the way there. That being said, for some reason I read the sign as I was entering the village backwards, and I thought the village was in the opposite direction that it was. I was driving for a ways before I decided I didn't know where I was going, but I saw 2 old ladies talking on the side of the road, so I pulled over, killed the engine, and started to ask for directions. I am so glad that I put in as much work as I have into learning Japanese. I am still basically useless, but I know enough to start a conversation, ask for directions, and end the conversation with a polite thank you. I was able to communicate well enough with these two to figure out pretty quickly that I was going in the wrong direction, and where I needed to go, without needing to use google maps.

![The Sakitsu Church, constructed of concrete and wood](/blog/images/sakitsuChurch.JPG)

Pulling into the village, I was flagged down by one of the visitor center managers, and she directed me to bike parking (No english, but I could still understand!). There's a weird dichotomy between there being lots of motorcycles, and motorcycle infrastructure, and the fact that motorcycles are not cars, and are not treated like cars. In the US, almost 100% of the time you park your motorcycle in the same spot you would park a car. The only difference is you can fit a few motorcycles into one car spot, but that's pretty rare. In Japan, you generally can't/shouldn't park a motorcycle where a car could park, but motorcycle parking isn't well defined, so it is quite tough, especially as a foreigner, to figure out what to do. Having this person reach out to help with the parking situation was a huge relief.

Actually going up to the church, at first glance, you wouldn't really think anything of it. It really just looks like a small/medium church, in a small town. However, this church has a very important story within Japanese Christianity, as it tells the story of the "Hidden Christians" of Japan, during the period of Japan where Christianity was persecuted and christians had to practice in hiding. 

There was a funeral going on at the church when I arrived, so I decided not to try to go in, so as not to bother anyone. There was a museum nearby, though not well marked, so I was quite lucky to run into it. Only 100 yen for the entry fee, and it included an IPad to use for english translations for the exhibits. I had a really nice time learning more about the Hidden Christian period in Japan, and the lengths that these christians would go to, to continue to practice their faith without being caught.

## Ferry to Kagoshima

After leaving Sakitsu village, I headed to the ferry that I would need to take to leave Amakusa and head to Kagoshima Prefecture. I stopped at a Family Mart just before the ferry to grab some cold ice cream, and I found an apple flavored coolish (single serve bag of ice cream). I parked the bike, was able to communicate to get a ticket, and waited in line, sipping on my cold ice cream.

While waiting for the ferry, 2 more motorcycles pulled up behind me. It seemed that they were riding together, and they were kind enough to stop and chat with me, about my bike, and the places that we had been. Kazu San was really keen on talking about motorcycle trips, and he was averaging ~400 KM / day on his bike, where I was averaging ~150 KM / day. What a trooper! I asked for his recommendation on bike roads to take within Kyushu, as I have had lots of success with Kyushu natives giving me good recommendations. Most of his recommendations I had heard of before, but he suggested a few places in mid/north Kyushu that I hadn't heard of, and he shared tons of pictures of his own travels across Kyushu. He owns 5 bikes, including a Ninja and a Maxi Scooter, which he was riding today.

## Where is my navigation taking me?

After getting off the ferry, I continued on to Izumi. My navigation app, which has been fantastic this entire trip, decided to take me on some of the most abandoned roads that I have ever been on. These roads had rocks all over the road, as big as my fist, and were covered with leaves, moss, and tree branches. I have no idea how many people ride on these roads anymore, and while they are cool to see a couple of times, today my navigation app just kept sending me down these roads, for 25 minutes at a time. 

The best way that I can describe the experience is akin to what it feels like to be riding a mountain bike down a technical downhill, where you have to choose your line carefully. I was basically doing this the entire time on these roads, but both uphill and downhill as I was on the bike. 

## Arrival in Izumi

As I began to arrive in Izumi, I realized it is in a very flat area of Japan. To see anything flat in Japan, after being in nothing but mountains and hills, is a nice reprieve. Ayase San said something about this last night, about Wisconsin, asking if it was very flat. I thought to myself at the time, "Who would want to experience something flat? Mountains are so beautiful, flat is just boring by comparison". 

However, I think I'm beginning to understand the sentiment that Ayase San was getting at. When you're in one environment for such a long time, you get tired of it. It doesn't really matter what the environment is, whether it's hot, cold, mountainous, flat, urban, or rural. Being in one environment for so long, you become accustomed to the environment. But when you transition quickly from that environment to a different one, there is contrast, which allows you to truly appreciate the beauty in the new space, and also begin to more truly appreciate the environment that you had become accustomed to.

Ayase also messaged me this morning, to note that what I had translated to "once-in-a-lifetime encounter" was expressed as "ichigo-ichie" (一期一会) in Japanese. I looked into it more on my own, and I learned a bit more of what it is intended to mean. Effectively, the term is used to express that encounters that you have with someone else will never happen in the same way and the same circumstances again, and that you should cherish the time you have with gratitude. This is something that I think I have wanted to do while on my trip here, but that I need to take home with me. I've been thinking about the meaning and use of this phrase all day, and how it's applied both to Japanese culture and how it can/should be applied to my interactions with others through life. I think that it's a really powerful idea, and I will continue to be thinking about it throughout my trip, and through all of the new and fleeting interactions that I have with people while I'm here.
