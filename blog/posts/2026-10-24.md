## Tokyo National Museum

I had a great time walking around, but it was raining, so I didn't really want to walk around outside for an extremely extended amount of time. Instead, I decided to walk straight to the museum. There were normal museum tickets, and 2 special exhibits. I decided to purchase a ticket to one of the special exhibits, which included a normal ticket as well. The normal museum was really cool, with lots of archeological artifacts, tons of scroll paintings & calligraphy, and a really cool hall full of swords of various types, including katanas, tanto blades, and one really old straight blade. Did you know, Japanese swords were almost all perfectly straight until the 11th century? I had no idea, since I so closely associate curved swords with Japan. I think of all of the individual exhibits within the museum, the swords were pretty close to the top of the list in terms of cool factor.

Throughout the museum, I really liked the architecture of the building, though it was less varied and styled than my favorite museums like the Nelson Atkins. 

![The main entrance of the Tokyo National Museum](/blog/images/nationalMuseumEntrance.JPG)

My favorite exhibits are almost always bodhisattva statues, made of wood. There were quite a few within this museum, but they offered different qualities than the ones that I'm used to seeing. In the Nelson Atkins, there's a massive bodhisattva statue, made of a single piece of wood. It's scaled to be just a bit larger than a human, so it has a real sense of presence and scale. The statues here though, they were mostly smaller than human scale, with the difference being in the quality of the preservation, and more ornate details. Most statues here had all of their fingers, their nose, and lots of ornate details remaining. It was really cool to see them on their original lotus base as well.

![A statue with red flames surrounding it. A bodhisattva can be seen in the display case reflection](/blog/images/tokyoMuseumFlames.JPG)

One big difference in this museum compared to a lot of other museums that I've been to, I'd say a solid 30% of the pieces were calligraphy. Obviously, in Japan, there's a whole culture surrounding calligraphy, but it does make it a bit more difficult to appreciate as a foreigner looking at the pieces, as I kind of just have to look at it and go "Those are some cool strokes". But, without being able to actually READ the words, that's about as far as it goes. A lot of the pieces that were kept for key historical significance were death poems, or ceremonial descriptions. There wasn't an English translation of the text sitting next to the piece (you'd also need Korean and Chinese), so the meaning and significance was somewhat lost on me unfortunately. 

One of the pieces in the archeological section was a Dogū. These little guys were made of clay, and had goggle looking eyes. I saw 2 or 3 of them in the museum, and I hadn't really seen these anywhere else. It was also odd, because I couldn't think of anything that these guys would be a reference to, like an animal, or a dragon, or a person, they were just a little Dogū. 

![A Dogū statue, from the Tokyo National Museum](/blog/images/tokyoMuseumDogu.JPG)

The special exhibit that I visited was all on the Daitokuji Temple, which is a Zen temple in Kyoto. The exhibit focused on how the temple was formed, how Zen came to be in Japan, and it traced through the lineage of Zen masters within the temple, showcasing many artifacts, and pieces of artwork that come from the temple itself. Basically the entire exhibit was a combination of 5 different types of artwork: Calligraphy, Scroll Portraits, Scroll paintings, Section Paintings, and Statues. However, the exhibit focused heavily on the Calligraphy and Scroll Portraits of the Zen masters. At the very end of the exhibit, they included some of the statues from the second floor of the temple, which have never been available to the public until this exhibition. The entire exhibit was no-photography. I'm guessing the reason for that is to give people a reason to still pay to attend the exhibit, but the big exception were the statues from the second floor of the temple, which were placed at the very end of the exhibit. 

![A triad of 3 statues, on a pedestal](/blog/images/tokyoMuseumTriad.JPG)

## Walking around Ueno Park

After finishing up with the museum, and snagging a couple of postcards from the gift shop, I continued to walk around Ueno park, starting with the Tokyo National Museum garden. It was a really cool garden, with lots of tea ceremony buildings throughout the garden, which were not for use, but donated to the museum from across the country, and fit the garden atmosphere well. The pond in the garden was full of ducks, and it was very peaceful with the light rain.

Continuing on from the garden, I explored a couple of the shrines that can be found in the park. The park is big enough that you can't really see one shrine from another, so it feels like each shrine is it's own thing. I've found that a bit disorienting about Japan in general, as it's so hilly, and so vertically built, the sight lines are very obscured, and you feel like you're in a more intimate encounter than the city appears from a satellite perspective.

The Lotus filled pond was really cool. Just a massive pond, filled to the brim with lotus flowers. Unfortunately it's not in season for the actual flowers, but it was cool to be able to look across a wide space, with no buildings incumbering the view.

![A picture of a temple, across a lotus filled pond](/blog/images/tokyoUenoPond.JPG)

As I was walking around the park, I stumbled across a performance hall. I had seen it from across the pond, but once I got to a sight line where I could see into the stage, I did a double take. "Is that the stage from the Tokyo exhibition match in Marty Supreme?" And yes. It was. Can't believe I was able to pull that one out of my brain.

![A badly framed picture of the Ueno Park stage](/blog/images/tokyoUenoStage.JPG)

## Shopping & Food

After exploring the park, I decided to go shopping. I had packed light for my entire motorcycle trip, but now was the time to load up on souvenirs, clothes, and whatever else I could find from Tokyo. The natural first stop? Uniqlo. I went in, and picked up 2 more pairs of my favorite pants, along with a pair of jeans (yes jeans, I'm working on it), and a cotton light blue striped button down. I also picked up a hat! I've been searching for a hat for this whole trip, and I found a brown corduroy peanuts woodstock hat. Super cute, and just Japanese enough to come away as a souvenir, but not so tacky that I won't be able to wear it back home. 

The price for Uniqlo clothing is just too good for the quality of the clothes. It's hard to believe how cheap it all is, and not having to pay tax on it is just one more insane advantage. And it's not like I'm shopping at Gap or something, Uniqlo clothing is actually very high quality. Sure, the jeans aren't going to be beating Iron Heart or something, but it's not throwaway junk like Shein either.

I did a bit more shopping/looking around in shops in the general area, but I didn't find anything I really liked or needed. There was a ticket based restaurant that I passed by earlier that looked pretty good, so I went back and checked it out. I got a pork tonkotsu dish, and a beer (sue me), for a total of 1030 yen. So cheap, and would've been only 630 yen without the beer. Crazy. And the dish was so good too, and came with a miso soup.
