# Japan Trip - Day 5

The views today were insane. So many different points to view across several islands, and to view across the ocean. I was absolutely floored by the whole trip, and the roads were still fantastic. 

I started the day in Sasebo. I walked around the harbor a bit, looking at the U.S. and Japan navy ships, which were HUGE. I wasn't able to fully see the ships, but it was enough to see the portinons of the ships that I did get to see.

![A panorama of the Sasebo Harbor, with Navy ships](/blog/images/saseboHarbor.jpg)

I went into a bookstore by the harbor, and got a souvenir for my mom. She was born in Sasebo, and wanted a souvenir from there. Really, she wanted a t-shirt that said "I was born in Sasebo, on the Naval base" but obviously I'm not gonna find that. So I got a t-shirt that said Sasebo on it.

## Biking!

The biking today was still so good. Lots of tunnels, and high bridges. I called my Mom early in the morning, as I was leaving Sasebo, and we talked for the first half hour of my ride. Most of the ride today was on open roads, with gaps in the trees that I could look out over to see out across the mountains.

Lots of switchbacks as well today, which is always super fun, but not too many switchbacks to make it annoying. As I was going along, I saw a sign with a "300m" on it pointing down a side road. There wasn't any english translation, and no pictures, but for some reason I felt called to head down the road and see what was there. Good thing too, because it ended up being a combination campground and scenic overlook!

![A panorama of a scenic overlook, somewhere between Sasebo and Nagasaki.](/blog/images/sasebo-nagasakiOverlook.jpg)

It was so pretty looking out over the bay (Omura Bay), and there was even a walking path to the side that I took some time to get off the bike and walk around. The walking path was so chill, and went around a little mini lake, which I think was a reservoir for a small town, since there were a lot of signs to not litter. There were also quite a few signs for snake hazard, maybe a poisonous snake issue?

![Sign for snake hazard, text in Japanese](/blog/images/snakeHazard.JPG)

While I was walking around, there were a few spider webs that I ran into, and when I took the time to look at the spiders, I realized they were HUGE! I tried to get a few good pictures of them, but probably didn't do the best job. Hopefully they're not dangerous? I didn't mess with them, they looked scary.

![A large japanese spider, in it's web](/blog/images/japaneseSpider1.JPG)

## Nagasaki

After some more biking, I made it to Nagasaki. I probably arrived in Nagasaki around 2pm, and had enough time to tour the Atomic Bomb museum. I looked online for parking, and there was a parking lot underneath the peace statue that had motorcycle accommodation. I went to the entrance, but the guy running it said I shouldn't go in, and should instead go to the exit, which was around the corner to the left (左). I made it to the exit, and found a worker who directed me to come in, make u-turn, and park right next to the exit gate, behind some cones. Odd... I did so, and then tried to pay, or get a ticket, but they both wouldn't give me a ticket, or accept payment. The guys I was talking to didn't speak much english (only a couple words) but the little they did speak was telling me to leave, then come back and pay. For whatever reason, I didn't believe that I was getting what they were saying correctly, even though I was, and so I spent probably an extra 2 minutes trying to confirm. 

![Peace statue, a blue statue of a man with one hand raised, and another outstretched](/blog/images/peaceStatue.JPG)

I did finally get off the bike, and was able to wak around the Atomic Bomb museum and memorial. Firstly, there were a TON of japanese students around. I don't know if it's always like this, or if there was something going on, but there were probably thousands of students in total, being led around in groups of 20-30 students. I started at the peace statue, pictured above, and then started walking towards the fountain of peace, and the Atomic Bomb epicenter. I walked down towards the stream that ran by the epicenter, since there were signs for it, and there was a piece of wall that was kept intact from the bombing, with a sign describing the significance of the stream.

This is where I started to get emotional. I have experienced war museums in the US, on WW1, WW2, even the Truman library, which has a large section on the atomic bombs. In each of these museums though, you're very isolated from the events that took place, just as Americans have been from most wars that the US has participated in, where the war happens overseas, and the consequences are only felt indirectly. Now though, I'm confronted by the results of the war, directly underneath where the bomb would have gone off. Looking around at the dense city surrounding me and the young students running around, I get a much different view. It was hard not to imagine the bomb going off today, to look around and see how much damage it would cause, and to think about the people standing next to me.

The atomic bomb museum was a very good museum, and I continued to feel emotional. I'm not one to tear up, or even generally feel emotional in a museum, but this museum did a very good job of showing the horrifying results of the nuclear bomb in Nagasaki. This museum felt much more focused on the direct felt outcomes of the bombing than the lead up to the bombing, and the greater war surrounding the bombing. The museum messaging was heavily focused on the message that we as a society should do whatever we can to ensure that nuclear weapons are never used again, and that they should be removed from society entirely. Towards the end of the museum there was a video talking about the efforts in the UN to add legislation to make the use of Atomic weapons fully illegal, and it seems like the law is still in progress, with some significant milestones hopefully coming in the next year. I will definitely be keeping an eye out to see what the result of these efforts ends up coming to.

## Hotel & Food

I didn't have a hotel picked out ahead of time for Nagasaki. I didn't have anything ahead of time for Sasebo either, but that worked out fine. Nagasaki was a different story, I think from a combination of factors, including the number of students in Nagasaki that day, and Nagasaki just being a higher tourism city. I tried to find a hotel, but ended up settling on JAL City, which turned out to be in Nagasaki Chinatown. Yes, chinatowns exist in Japan. I paid significantly more than I had so far for this hotel, but it wasn't unreasonable for the hotels around Nagasaki. 

Also, it was unreasonably hard to find motorcycle parking. All of the paid parking lots for cars don't allow motorcycles, so you have to find motorcycle specific parking, or risk it and just park on the sidewalk. That's what I did in Sasebo, since there were already a few other bikes nearby, but I couldn't find anything like that in Nagasaki. 

I looked for a good spot for food while chilling in the hotel, and found a Ramen spot that was highly recommended. Most ramen places that I've been by run on a ticket machine, so you order at a machine, and it'll spit out a ticket that you then give to the kitchen staff, and sit wherever you want. This place had 4 main ramen options at the top, and just as I was about to hit the spicy ramen option, I saw a row below labeled "Beer combo". Easy choice. 

![A ramen shop booth, with an Asahi Super dry, ramen bowl, and pork bowl](/blog/images/ramenNagasaki.JPG)

The combo came with a pork bowl that came out really quickly, which was a fantastic appetizer, and pairing with the Asahi Super Dry that came out. The Ramen was quite spicy, and very rich, and overall, the best meal that I've had so far. 

I had dropped my clothes off at a coin laundry before going to get ramen, so I stopped at a 711 to get some ice cream, and a drink for the night, and then picked up my clothes and went back to the hotel. I was very tired, so I'm actually writing this the day after.
